Tea refers to the evergreen shrub Camellia sinensis, to the brewed beverage
made from its leaves, and to the social ritual of taking tea. In China, where
tea drinking is documented for well over a thousand years, it was tied to
medicine, Buddhist practice, and refined connoisseurship.

Tea reached Europe in the early seventeenth century, first through Dutch
traders and then in large volumes through the English East India Company. By
the 1690s it was sold in New England. In eighteenth-century Britain tea shifted
from an expensive luxury to a daily drink across social classes.

Its reception was contested. Promoters advertised tea as healthful and
sobering. Critics disputed this: the merchant Jonas Hanway, in a 1756 essay,
claimed tea caused physical deterioration and was ruinous to the labouring
poor, while others attacked it on moral and economic grounds. These were
opposing viewpoints on the same drink, expressed in the same decades.

The tea trade reshaped politics. To pay for Chinese tea, for which Britain had
few goods China wanted, the East India Company expanded opium exports from
India to China, a trade that led to the Opium Wars in the mid-nineteenth
century. In North America, the tax on imported tea provoked the Boston Tea
Party of 1773. From the 1830s the British established tea plantations in Assam
and Ceylon, moving cultivation out of China and into the colonial empire.
