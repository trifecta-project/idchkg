In the early-modern spice trade, "spices" meant above all pepper, and the fine
spices nutmeg, mace, and cloves. Nutmeg and mace both come from a single tree,
Myristica fragrans, native to the Banda Islands in the Moluccas; cloves came
from a handful of neighbouring islands. For centuries these were the only
places on earth where the trees grew.

Medieval Europe received spices through a long chain of Arab, Indian, and
Venetian intermediaries, at high prices. From 1498 the Portuguese, having
reached India by sea, sought to control the source and the routes, seizing
Malacca in 1511 and reaching the Spice Islands soon after.

In the seventeenth century the Dutch East India Company (VOC) fought to turn
access into monopoly. To fix the price of nutmeg the VOC took control of the
Banda Islands in 1621; the campaign under Jan Pieterszoon Coen depopulated much
of the archipelago through killing, expulsion, and enslavement. Company
directors presented the monopoly as prudent commercial management. Later
historians describe the same events as a colonial atrocity committed to protect
a trade good.

Spices illustrate how value and identity shift over time: nutmeg moved from a
priceless rarity, once exchanged for its weight in gold, to an ordinary
commodity once cultivation spread. In the eighteenth century French and British
smugglers transplanted seedlings to Mauritius, Grenada, and Zanzibar, breaking
the monopoly and collapsing the extraordinary prices.
