Sugar names several things at once: the crop sugarcane (Saccharum
officinarum), the refined crystalline sweetener extracted from it, and, in
early-modern commerce, a colonial commodity so lucrative it was called "white
gold".

Sugarcane was domesticated in New Guinea and spread across South and Southeast
Asia; Arab agriculturalists carried its cultivation and refining westward
around the Mediterranean by the medieval period. In the fifteenth and
sixteenth centuries the Portuguese and Spanish established cane plantations on
Atlantic islands such as Madeira and São Tomé, and then across the Caribbean
and Brazil.

From the seventeenth century, sugar production in the Caribbean was organised
around large plantations worked by enslaved Africans. Contemporary planters
and mercantilist writers described sugar as an engine of national wealth and a
mark of refined taste. British abolitionists in the late eighteenth century
argued the opposite: that every pound of West Indian sugar was, as the
campaigners put it, produced by coerced labour, and they organised consumer
boycotts of slave-grown sugar in the 1790s.

Historians today treat the sugar economy as inseparable from Atlantic slavery
and the plantation system, and as a driver of the triangular trade that moved
manufactured goods, enslaved people, and sugar between Europe, Africa, and the
Americas. After abolition, cane sugar faced competition from European beet
sugar, developed in the early nineteenth century, which shifted production
geography again.
