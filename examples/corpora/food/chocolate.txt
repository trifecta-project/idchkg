Chocolate is made from the seeds of the cacao tree, Theobroma cacao, native to
the Amazon basin. The word can name the raw cacao bean, the ground paste, the
drink, and the later solid confection - distinct forms often flattened into one
term.

In Mesoamerica cacao was prepared as a frothed, usually unsweetened and spiced
drink, and served ritual and monetary as well as culinary roles: among the
Aztecs cacao beans functioned as currency. Spanish colonisers encountered the
drink in the sixteenth century and carried cacao to Europe, where it was
sweetened with sugar and, for a long period, consumed as a fashionable hot
beverage of the wealthy.

Contemporary European writers praised chocolate as nourishing and even
medicinal; churchmen debated whether the drink broke religious fasts, an
argument about its very nature. Production depended on cacao plantations in the
Caribbean, coastal Venezuela, and later West Africa, worked in many periods by
enslaved and then coerced labour - a colonial provenance the finished luxury
concealed.

Chocolate changed form again in the nineteenth century. Dutch processing (the
cocoa press of 1828) separated cocoa butter from powder, and manufacturers in
Britain and Switzerland developed the solid eating chocolate and, later, milk
chocolate that displaced the drink as the dominant form. What "chocolate" meant
had shifted from a spiced Mesoamerican beverage to an industrial confection.
