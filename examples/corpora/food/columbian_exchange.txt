The Columbian Exchange is the name historians give to the transfer of plants,
animals, people, and diseases between the Americas and the rest of the world
following 1492. The term was popularised by the historian Alfred Crosby in
1972.

Crops moved in both directions. Maize, potatoes, tomatoes, chilli peppers,
cassava, and cacao travelled from the Americas to Europe, Africa, and Asia;
wheat, rice, sugarcane, coffee, and citrus, along with cattle, pigs, and
horses, moved the other way. The potato, native to the Andes, became a staple
across northern Europe over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and
transformed diets and populations - its later failure in the 1840s helped cause
the Irish famine.

The exchange was not benign. Old World diseases such as smallpox, to which
Indigenous American populations had no immunity, caused catastrophic mortality
and are considered a major cause of demographic collapse in the Americas.

Interpretations differ by viewpoint. Earlier accounts often framed the exchange
as agricultural enrichment and progress. Later scholarship emphasises the human
cost - depopulation, coerced labour, and the entanglement of new crops with
plantation slavery - arguing that the movement of foods cannot be separated from
colonial conquest. The same chilli pepper that became essential to cuisines in
India, Sichuan, and West Africa arrived through the same networks that carried
conquest and disease.
