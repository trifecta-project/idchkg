The transatlantic trade of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries is often
described as a triangular trade. In the simplified model, manufactured goods
went from European ports to West Africa; enslaved Africans were carried across
the Atlantic in the Middle Passage to the Americas; and plantation commodities
- sugar, tobacco, coffee, and cotton - were shipped back to Europe.

The reality was messier than a neat triangle, but the human core is not in
doubt: an estimated twelve million or more Africans were forcibly embarked, and
well over a million died during the Middle Passage. Ports such as Liverpool,
Bristol, Nantes, and Lisbon grew wealthy on the trade, financing shipping,
insurance, and banking.

Contemporaries argued over it in their own time. Merchants and planters defended
the slave trade as legal, profitable, and, they claimed, necessary to the
colonies. Abolitionists - among them formerly enslaved writers such as Olaudah
Equiano, whose narrative appeared in 1789 - condemned it as a crime, and
organised petitions and boycotts. Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807 and
slavery in most of its empire in 1833; other powers abolished at different
dates.

The maritime commodity trades of food history and the Atlantic slave trade were
thus one interlocking system: the sugar and coffee that reached European tables
moved on the same ships and through the same ports as the coerced labour that
produced them.
