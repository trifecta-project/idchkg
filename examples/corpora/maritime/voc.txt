The Dutch East India Company, known by its Dutch initials VOC (Vereenigde
Oostindische Compagnie), was chartered in 1602. It is often described as the
first company to issue shares to the public and as one of the earliest
multinational corporations. The name "VOC", however, covers two very different
identities: a trading company answering to shareholders, and a colonial state
that waged war, minted coin, signed treaties, and governed territory under its
charter.

The States General granted the VOC a monopoly on Dutch trade east of the Cape
of Good Hope. To secure the fine-spice trade the company established fortified
posts across the Indian Ocean and the Indonesian archipelago, founding Batavia
(modern Jakarta) as its Asian headquarters in 1619. To enforce its nutmeg
monopoly it seized the Banda Islands in 1621, an act that depopulated much of
the archipelago.

Views of the company diverge sharply. In older Dutch national memory the VOC
figured as an emblem of a commercial golden age and maritime enterprise. Recent
historians stress its violence, its role in slavery within Asia, and its
character as a colonial power, and public institutions in the Netherlands have
reassessed how it is commemorated - a clear case of a concept's meaning changing
over time.

Chronically indebted and mismanaged in its final decades, the VOC was
nationalised and dissolved by the Dutch state in 1799, its possessions passing
to the colonial government of the Dutch East Indies.
