The Age of Sail is the loosely bounded period, roughly from the sixteenth to
the mid-nineteenth century, in which international trade and naval power
depended on sailing ships. Its beginning is sometimes tied to the European
oceanic voyages of the late fifteenth century and its end to the rise of the
steamship.

The word "ship" in this period covers a changing cast of vessel types, and the
identity of the dominant ship evolved. The carrack and the galleon carried the
early Iberian trades. The heavily built East Indiaman served the chartered
companies. In the mid-nineteenth century the clipper, built for speed, carried
tea from China and, later, wool and passengers, before it was displaced in
turn. A "first-rate" in the same era named a warship of the largest class, a
quite different meaning of the same maritime vocabulary.

Long-distance sail depended on wind systems and on advances in navigation. The
problem of finding longitude at sea was not reliably solved until the marine
chronometer of the eighteenth century, associated with John Harrison, was
combined with lunar-distance methods.

The transition to steam was gradual and contested. Early steamships were
described by some contemporaries as unreliable and coal-hungry compared with
proven sail, while proponents stressed their independence from the wind. By the
late nineteenth century steam had displaced sail on most major routes, though
sailing vessels persisted in bulk trades into the twentieth century - an
identity that faded rather than ceased at a single moment.
