The Hanseatic League was a commercial and defensive confederation of merchant
guilds and market towns that dominated trade in the Baltic and North Seas from
the thirteenth to the fifteenth centuries, and lingered into the seventeenth.
The name "Hansa" referred first to associations of travelling merchants and only
later to the league of cities itself - the term's referent changed over time.

Centred on Lübeck and including towns such as Hamburg, Bremen, Danzig, and Reval,
the league linked the grain, timber, furs, wax, and amber of the east with the
cloth and manufactured goods of the west, and it controlled much of the trade in
Baltic herring and, later, salted fish. Its characteristic vessel was the cog, a
broad, sturdy cargo ship suited to bulk goods.

The Hansa maintained fortified trading posts, called Kontore, in foreign cities,
the most important being the Steelyard in London, Bryggen in Bergen, and the
Peterhof in Novgorod. These enclaves enjoyed extensive privileges negotiated with
local rulers.

The league had no single capital, army, or navy of its own in the modern sense,
and historians debate whether to call it a state, an alliance, or simply a
network - a genuine question of identity. It declined as territorial states such
as the emerging Dutch Republic, England, and the Scandinavian kingdoms asserted
control over their own trade, and as the Atlantic economy shifted the centre of
European commerce westward.
