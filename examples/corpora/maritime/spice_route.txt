The maritime spice routes were the sea lanes that carried spices, and much else,
across the Indian Ocean between East Asia, South Asia, the Middle East, and East
Africa, and onward to Europe. They long predate European involvement: monsoon
winds, whose seasonal reversal was understood by Arab, Indian, and Malay
navigators, made regular open-sea crossings possible, and ports such as Calicut,
Malacca, Aden, and Hormuz functioned as cosmopolitan entrepôts.

Control of these routes changed hands repeatedly. Before 1500 the trade was
shared among many merchant communities, with Venice dominating the final leg
into Europe. From 1498 Portugal, under the banner of the Estado da Índia,
attempted to redirect the trade by force, seizing choke points including Goa
(1510), Malacca (1511), and Hormuz (1515), and demanding that Asian shipping buy
passes.

The Portuguese never fully monopolised the trade, and in the seventeenth century
the Dutch and English companies displaced them from many positions. The same
port city could therefore hold several identities in succession - an independent
sultanate, a Portuguese fortress, a Dutch company town - as sovereignty passed
between powers.

Perspectives on the routes differ. In one telling they are a story of
navigation, exchange, and the meeting of cultures across a connected ocean world.
In another they are a record of armed commerce, in which European powers used
naval violence to extract a share of trades that had flourished without them.
